# AI Writing Signals

Use this reference to diagnose and review prose, not as a banned-word list. An expression is a problem only when it does not carry necessary information, conflicts with the author's or brand's voice, or clusters locally. Prefer adding facts and restructuring information over synonym replacement.

## 1. Unsupported certainty

**Signal:** A claim is strong but lacks scope, conditions, or evidence.

- “Dramatically improves efficiency,” “industry-leading,” or “a better experience for every team.”
- “Reliable,” “comprehensive,” “seamless,” “powerful,” or “transformative” stands in for an observable outcome.

**Response:** State the subject, action, result, conditions, and evidence. Instead of “improves collaboration,” write “Administrators can view pending invitations on one page and revoke them individually.” Narrow or remove the claim when reliable evidence is unavailable.

## 2. Abstraction and noun stacks

**Signal:** A sentence is grammatically correct but cannot answer “what concretely happens?”

- “Empower teams to build an end-to-end collaboration loop.”
- “Upgrade capabilities to create a more intelligent workflow.”

**Response:** Restore subjects and verbs. Say who does what, where, what result they see, and why it matters. Do not use abstractions such as “empower,” “enable,” “synergy,” “closed loop,” “end-to-end,” “upgrade,” or “capability building” in place of behavior.

## 3. Formulaic openings, transitions, and endings

**Signal:** The text opens, connects, or concludes with a sentence that would suit almost any subject.

- “In today’s fast-paced world,” “As [the field] continues to evolve.”
- “It is worth noting,” “At its core,” “That said,” “Not only … but also …”
- “In conclusion,” “By doing X, you can Y.”

**Response:** Start with the product change, reader problem, concrete scenario, direct conclusion, or necessary constraint. Keep a transition only when it communicates a real causal, contrasting, or chronological relationship. End with a decision, action, or unresolved question rather than a summary of the body.

## 4. Formulaic rhetoric and excessive symmetry

**Signal:** Symmetrical lists of three, parallel constructions, rhetorical questions, metaphors, or “Whether you are A, B, or C” appear mainly to make prose sound fluent.

**Response:** Retain rhetoric only when it improves understanding or recall. Break apart unnecessary parallel items and order paragraphs by information dependency rather than visual completeness. Vary sentence length naturally; do not manufacture alternation for its own sake.

## 5. Fillers, academic padding, and corporate language

**Signal:** An intensifier or formal phrase does not change meaning.

- “Actually,” “basically,” “clearly,” “certainly,” “truly,” “very,” or “significantly.”
- “Based on this,” “in terms of,” “at the level of,” or “it deserves attention that.”
- Repeated `leverage`, `utilize`, `facilitate`, `robust`, `seamless`, or `comprehensive`.

**Response:** Remove words that add no information. When formal tone is required, use shorter, direct language. Do not weaken technical terminology, legal terms, or risk disclosure merely to sound natural.

## 6. False completeness

**Signal:** The text lists benefits without prerequisites, costs, limits, compatibility, failure paths, or uncertainty; or it presents planned capability as shipped capability.

**Response:** Add the decision-relevant information for the situation. Documentation needs prerequisites and troubleshooting; release notes need affected users, migration, and known limits; marketing needs the applicable audience and defensible boundaries.

## 7. Missing voice, experience, and point of view

**Signal:** Every paragraph is equally polite, balanced, and abstract, with no real friction, trade-off, judgment, or verifiable detail.

**Response:** Use the most useful material from real product decisions, reader tasks, failure modes, examples, data, or customer language. Opinion writing should state a position and its scope. Documentation should not sacrifice clarity or accuracy in pursuit of personality.

## 8. Punctuation, layout, and translation residue

**Signal:** Em dashes, parentheses, colons, bold text, or lists are used frequently to manufacture rhythm; every paragraph is one sentence; or a translation retains the source language’s syntax and rhetoric.

**Response:** Let punctuation and layout serve information hierarchy. An em dash should be rare, deliberate emphasis—not a replacement for commas or a new sentence. Localize by rewriting in the target language; preserve intent and facts, not source sentence structure.

## Self-Check Questions

1. Does the core information remain if adjectives, intensifiers, and transitions are removed?
2. Does every paragraph add a fact, evidence, judgment, condition, or action?
3. Does the text state the applicable audience, prerequisites, limits, or next step?
4. Would a reader believe this was written by someone who understands the product and situation? Why?
5. Are there clusters of repeated sentence shapes, similar lengths, stock openings, or vocabulary?
